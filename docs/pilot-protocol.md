# Pilot protocol (draft)

**A cluster randomized trial of a brief low-information differential diagnosis
exercise in preclinical medical education**

Status: draft for course director and IRB review. Bracketed items are still
open. Nothing in this document should be treated as approved.

---

## 1. Background and rationale

Preclinical students are usually taught to reason after they have been given a
complete case. Real first encounters are the opposite. The clinician has a
complaint, very little else, and has to generate a differential anyway.

First Thought gives students repeated short practice at that specific moment.
The student sees a chief complaint with deliberately insufficient information,
writes an unaided differential in four minutes, and afterwards finds out
specifically what they named and what they missed.

### A note on what this tool is and is not

The tool contains no generative model. It scores free text against a curated
table of diagnosis names using exact term matching, and it never produces a
differential for the student. This is a design commitment, not a limitation.

The prevailing concern about digital tools in medical education is deskilling:
that a tool which supplies the answer removes the cognitive work that produces
the skill. First Thought is built the opposite way round. **The machine
withholds until the student commits.** The student must generate first, alone,
under time pressure, before any feedback exists.

This study therefore tests a design principle rather than a technology. If the
result is positive, the claim it supports is that generation-first digital
feedback improves reasoning. It does not support a claim about generative AI,
and the writeup must not make one.

## 2. Objectives

**Primary.** Does 16 to 20 repetitions of a four minute generation-first
differential exercise improve the quality of the differential students produce
on an unaided end-of-block OSCE post-encounter note?

**Secondary.**
1. Is the effect present at the mid-block formative OSCE?
2. Does the effect differ between MS1 and MS2 students?
3. Do in-app measures improve over the block, and does that trajectory predict
   OSCE performance?
4. Is the exercise feasible and acceptable to students and facilitators?

**Exploratory.** Students' self-reported use of generative AI tools in their own
studying, and its relationship to unaided reasoning performance at the OSCE.
This is observational and confounded, and is reported as hypothesis generating
only. It is included because the deskilling debate in medical education is
currently being conducted with almost no data.

## 3. Design

Two arm, parallel group, cluster randomized trial with the facilitator as a
matched block.

Each of the 22 facilitators carries a longitudinal cohort and meets their MS1
group on Tuesday morning and their MS2 group on Wednesday morning. These are
separate sessions with separate students.

**Each facilitator delivers the exercise to one of their two groups and runs the
other as usual.** Which class year receives it is randomized, so roughly half
the facilitators run it with their MS1 group and half with their MS2 group.

This is better than assigning whole facilitators to an arm. Every facilitator
appears in both arms, so differences in facilitator skill cancel out of the
comparison instead of adding noise. It also doubles the number of independent
groups per arm, from 11 to 22. Because the direction of assignment is
randomized, arm and class year are balanced across the whole design even though
they are paired within each facilitator.

Randomizing individual students was rejected because students in a small group
share materials.

## 4. Participants

- Setting: Becoming a Physician, [Institution]
- Population: all MS1 and MS2 students enrolled in the course
- Facilitators: 22, each meeting an MS1 group and an MS2 group on separate days
- Groups: 44, about 6 students each
- Approximate total: 264 students

## 5. Randomization

22 facilitators are randomized 1:1 to deliver the exercise either to their MS1
group or to their MS2 group. This yields 22 intervention groups and 22 control
groups, balanced by class year.

Allocation is generated by [the assessment office or an independent
statistician] using a fixed seed, recorded before the block begins, and not
revealed to the investigator until assignment is complete.

## 6. Intervention

Intervention groups run one four minute repetition per session, 16 to 20 times
across the block.

**Nothing is displayed to the room during the session.** This is deliberate. It
removes every point of technical failure from the classroom and means the
exercise can run with a phone and a printed page.

Each repetition:
1. The facilitator reads or displays a synthetic chief complaint at the level
   set for that class year. MS1 groups use the M1 and M2 information levels, MS2
   groups use M2 and M3.
2. Students write a differential through a Qualtrics link on their own device,
   unaided, within four minutes. They enter their study number, not their name.
3. The facilitator debriefs for one to two minutes from a printed answer key,
   which lists the can't-miss diagnoses, the commonly missed ones, and one
   teaching pearl.

**Individual feedback is returned at the next session.** Responses are scored
after the session by the study team using the term matcher. At the start of the
following week's session the facilitator hands back a slip to each student,
labelled with their study number only, showing what that student named and what
they missed on the previous case.

This step matters more than it looks. Retrieval practice produces most of its
benefit when the learner finds out specifically what *they* got wrong, not what
the group as a whole got wrong. A generic answer key is a substantially weaker
intervention, and a weaker intervention is harder to detect. Returning slips a
week later also spaces the feedback, which is itself well evidenced.

Only the student knows which number is theirs. The slips carry no names.

Total added time is about six minutes per session, plus one minute to hand back
slips, or under two and a half hours across the whole block.

**Control** groups continue Becoming a Physician as usual. Control students take
the same baseline task and the same OSCEs, and receive no slips.

## 7. Baseline

**All students.** Before the first session, every student in both arms completes
one written differential task through Qualtrics. Ten minutes, one case, unaided,
scored identically to the outcome. This is not a teaching activity.

It does three jobs. It raises power for an effect of 0.40 from about 76 percent
to between 80 and 90 percent depending on how well it predicts the outcome. It
lets any chance imbalance between arms be detected and adjusted for rather than
argued about afterwards. And it provides the first estimate of the intracluster
correlation, which is currently a planning assumption rather than a measurement.

**MS2 students additionally.** Every MS2 student already has a full set of OSCE
scores from their MS1 year, collected before this study existed and therefore
free of any influence from it. For the MS2 half of the sample this is a second
and stronger baseline, and it supports a within-student change analysis that is
more sensitive than comparing groups cross-sectionally.

Prior OSCE performance is used as an additional covariate in the MS2 stratum and
in a prespecified MS2 subgroup analysis. It is not available for MS1 students,
so the week-one task remains the common baseline across both years.

## 8. Outcomes

**Primary outcome.** Differential diagnosis quality on the free-text
post-encounter note of the end-of-block summative OSCE, expressed as the
proportion of the expert can't-miss list the student named unaided.

**Secondary outcomes.**
- The same score at the mid-block formative OSCE
- Number of distinct diagnoses named
- Number of distinct organ systems represented
- Change from the baseline task
- In-app trajectory across the block (intervention arm only)
- Student and facilitator acceptability survey at end of block
- Process measures: proportion of planned sessions delivered, proportion of
  students submitting per session, facilitator adherence to the script

## 9. Scoring and blinding

Students and facilitators cannot be blinded to arm.

Outcome scoring is performed by the same deterministic term matcher used in the
classroom display, applied to the free-text differential from every
post-encounter note against an expert key for that OSCE case. The algorithm has
no access to arm assignment, so outcome scoring is blind by construction and
identical across students.

**Validation of the algorithmic score.** A random sample of 60 post-encounter
notes, stratified by arm, is independently scored by a trained human rater who
is blinded to arm, using a written rubric. Agreement between the human and
algorithmic scores is reported. If agreement is inadequate [threshold to be
prespecified], human scoring becomes primary.

This step exists because the intervention and the outcome are scored by the same
method, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the study measured reasoning or
measured students learning to write lists the scorer likes.

## 10. Sample size

Two-sided alpha 0.05, 22 groups per arm, 6 students per group, 132 students per
arm.

These figures ignore the precision gained by blocking facilitator out of the
comparison, so they are conservative. Blocking can only help.

| Within-group ICC | Effective n per arm | Minimum detectable effect | Power at d = 0.40 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.05 | 106 | 0.39 | 83% |
| 0.10 | 88 | 0.42 | 76% |
| 0.15 | 75 | 0.46 | 69% |

Planning assumption is an ICC of 0.10, giving 76 percent power for an effect of
0.40, or roughly 80 to 90 percent once the baseline task is adjusted for.

**The two largest uncertainties are the ICC and the true effect size**, and the
second is now the more important of the two. Because the session itself shows
nothing to the room, the strength of the intervention depends almost entirely on
the individual feedback slips returned the following week.

| If the true effect is | Power at ICC 0.10 |
| --- | --- |
| 0.50 | 91% |
| 0.40 | 76% |
| 0.30 | 51% |
| 0.25 | 38% |

A version of the exercise that returned no individual feedback, only a spoken
answer key, would plausibly sit in the bottom two rows. That is the reasoning
behind section 6 and it should not be dropped for convenience.

Both quantities will be estimated from the baseline task and the first few
sessions, and reported regardless of the result. If the observed ICC exceeds
0.15, the study is underpowered for a realistic effect and a null result must be
reported as inconclusive rather than negative.

Two alternatives were considered and rejected:

- **A third arm receiving generative AI feedback.** This would push the minimum
  detectable effect to 0.55 to 0.60 after correcting for multiple comparisons.
  It belongs in a separate study.
- **Including MS3 and MS4 students.** Each facilitator also carries MS3 and MS4
  groups. Rejected because the OSCE outcome is not comparable across preclinical
  and clerkship years.

## 11. Analysis

Primary analysis is a mixed effects linear model with the OSCE differential
score as the outcome; arm, class year, and baseline score as fixed effects; and
facilitator as a random intercept. Because each facilitator contributes one
intervention group and one control group in opposite class years, the arm effect
is estimated within facilitator and facilitator variance drops out of the
comparison. Both intracluster correlations are reported.

**Small-sample correction is required.** With 22 clusters per arm nested in 22
facilitator blocks, standard mixed-model inference produces confidence intervals
that are too narrow. Degrees of freedom use the Kenward-Roger approximation, and
the primary result is confirmed with a permutation test that permutes the
direction of assignment within facilitators. Both are prespecified.

Analysis is by intention to treat: every consented student in a randomized group
is analysed in the arm to which their group was assigned, regardless of how many
sessions they attended.

Prespecified secondary analyses: the MS2 stratum with prior-year OSCE score as
an additional covariate; a within-student change analysis in MS2 students; and
the mid-block formative OSCE as a repeated measure.

Sensitivity analyses: cluster-level summary analysis, and a per-protocol
analysis restricted to students attending at least [threshold] sessions.

Missing outcomes are reported by arm. No imputation unless missingness exceeds
[threshold], in which case multiple imputation is used and reported.


## 12. Data handling and confidentiality

Students submit through Qualtrics, which is already licensed, security reviewed,
and backed up by the institution. No student data is stored by the application.

Each student is assigned a study ID by the assessment office. The crosswalk
between study ID and student identity is held by the assessment office and is
never shared with the investigator. The investigator receives only files keyed
by study ID.

Qualtrics attaches IP address, location, and recipient email to exports by
default. The ingestion tool removes all of these before any analysis file is
produced, and reports what it removed.

No protected health information is used. All cases are synthetic.

## 13. Consent, ethics, and student protection

The exercise runs for the whole small group as part of scheduled course time,
because it is a teaching activity in a required course. What is voluntary is
consent to use a student's data in the analysis. A student who declines still
participates in the activity with their group; their data is simply excluded.

Participation, declining, and performance in the exercise have no effect on any
grade or on standing in the course. The exercise is never graded.

IRB determination will be sought before any data is collected. The study is
expected to meet the criteria for exempt educational research, but that
determination belongs to the IRB and not to the investigator.

## 14. Answer keys

Every can't-miss list, important-miss list, and teaching pearl is reviewed and
signed off before use by three emergency physicians and at least one faculty
member who teaches in Becoming a Physician. A diagnosis stays on a can't-miss
list only if at least two of three reviewers agree. Disagreements are recorded
rather than quietly resolved. See `answer-key-review.md`.

The draft cases were written from an emergency medicine perspective and must be
checked against what the course is actually teaching in each week.

## 15. Conflicts of interest

The investigator developed the software and teaches at the institution. The
investigator does not deliver the intervention to any group, does not score any
outcome, and does not hold the study ID crosswalk. There is no commercial
interest in the software and no intent to monetize it.

## 17. Limitations

- Single institution, single course
- Participants and facilitators cannot be blinded
- The algorithmic outcome score shares its method with the intervention, which
  the human validation sample partially but not fully addresses
- **Contamination is a larger risk here than it would be for a software based
  intervention, not a smaller one.** Because nothing is displayed in the room,
  the intervention is essentially a way of running four minutes of a session.
  A facilitator cannot accidentally show an app that is never shown, but they
  can very easily start asking their control group what dangerous diagnosis they
  are missing, because that is simply good teaching. This biases toward finding
  nothing, so it makes a positive result more credible and a null result harder
  to interpret. It is measured two ways: control students are asked at the end
  of the block whether they encountered the exercise, and facilitators are asked
  directly whether they carried anything across, framed as helping the study
  rather than as a compliance check.
- The exploratory AI-use analysis is observational and cannot support causal
  claims
